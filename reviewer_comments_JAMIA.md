## Comments to the Author

This perspective presents an interesting attempt to characterize the evolution of healthcare IT and propose "computable clinical intent" as a future research direction. The idea that clinical intent could be operationalized through standards such as FHIR resources (e.g., Task, ServiceRequest, CarePlan) is thought-provoking and potentially important.

However, the paper's opening premise is not sufficiently supported (particularly by the first 2 references). The authors argue that the evolution of healthcare IT can be characterized by ... technology adoption, yet the cited references do not adequately justify this claim. More importantly, this framing overlooks the socio-technical nature of biomedical and health informatics as a field, including its theories, methods, standards, and subdomains. The manuscript often conflates healthcare IT with informatics, which is the audience of this journal.

A major limitation is the lack of clarity regarding the problem being solved and the intended audience. The abstract states an objective to "organize" healthcare IT, but it remains unclear whether the proposed framework is intended as a conceptual model for informatics research, a roadmap for health system IT functions, or a set of functional requirements for future clinical systems. The terms 'Actionable Clinical Record' (ACR) and 'computable clinical intent' are introduced, but their practical applications are not described.

The discussion of FHIR-based representations of clinical intent is intriguing, but the paper does not adequately present the current state of FHIR adoption, implementation challenges, or the role of unstructured data sources such as clinical notes. In addition, many informatics practitioners would challenge the assertion that patient records and clinical facts are already 'broadly computable' in routine healthcare settings.

The conclusion proposes "patient-specific clinical intent as the next layer of computability in healthcare" and introduces the ACR and its evaluation framework as reusable constructs. While this vision is forward-looking, the concepts remain too abstract to provide actionable value for readers. More concrete examples, use cases, and implications are needed.

Overall, the manuscript presents an innovative idea and suggests a potentially useful vocabulary for relating clinical actions to intent. However, the framework is not yet sufficiently developed to demonstrate relevance, utility, or impact for the JAMIA readership.

In its current form, the submission also is misaligned with JAMIA's guidance for Perspective articles, which are intended to "provide views on issues of broad importance to informatics readers, including policies, major initiatives, and future directions with clear implications for the field."
