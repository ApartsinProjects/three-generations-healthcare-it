## 1. Recommendation — Major revision

I recommend **Major revision**. The manuscript has a potentially useful conceptual core—the distinction between computable clinical state and computable patient-specific intended action—but the current “third generation of healthcare IT” framing is substantially stronger than the evidence and conceptual analysis presently support. The most important problem is not the absence of a new experiment, which is acceptable for a Perspective, but that the manuscript has not yet demonstrated that the ACR represents a sufficiently distinct computational object rather than a useful intermediate representation for populating existing workflow constructs such as FHIR Task, ServiceRequest, and CarePlan. A publishable revision should narrow several categorical claims, sharpen the boundary with existing informatics constructs, and make the proposed framework more obviously reusable and falsifiable rather than primarily terminological.

## 2. Significance and novelty for biomedical informatics; reusability of the ACR

The central problem is significant: clinically consequential intentions often remain embedded in communication even when diagnoses, observations, and orders are otherwise represented computationally, and the manuscript usefully focuses attention on converting such communication into auditable workflow objects. The **ACR tuple—action, target, actor, temporal constraint, condition, dependency, status, provenance, confidence—is plausible as a research abstraction**, particularly because provenance, temporal semantics, uncertainty, and lifecycle status are explicitly incorporated rather than treating the task as ordinary information extraction. However, the manuscript currently asserts novelty more than it establishes it: several fields have close analogues in workflow standards, task representations, guideline formalisms, clinical NLP relation extraction, and care-plan representations, so the paper must explain precisely what is conceptually new about the ACR rather than simply what is assembled within it. I could see researchers reusing the ACR if the authors provide explicit semantics, cardinalities, lifecycle rules, examples of revisions/cancellations/conflicting instructions, and a mapping to FHIR resources; without that formalization, “ACR” risks becoming a new label for an action-oriented extraction schema rather than a durable informatics construct.

## 3. Soundness of the conceptual framework

The prescribed/observed/intended distinction is the strongest conceptual contribution because it identifies genuinely different epistemic sources: normative recommendations, retrospectively observed events, and patient-specific commitments or plans. However, the paper overstates the boundary when it says that existing paradigms address the first two whereas the third layer uniquely addresses intended process: FHIR explicitly represents patient-specific intent once structured, and clinical systems already contain orders, tasks, appointments, care plans, medication plans, and conditional instructions that embody intended future actions. The defensible claim is narrower and more interesting: **existing standards can represent intent, but they do not themselves recover incompletely expressed patient-specific intent from heterogeneous natural clinical communication**; the manuscript should use this formulation consistently. The three-generation model is also more heuristic than demonstrated: the five proposed markers do not establish that “record → state → intent” constitutes historically discrete computational generations, and the statement that each layer “subsumes” the previous one is particularly questionable because storing records, representing facts, and managing workflow are overlapping capabilities rather than strict nested abstractions. The framework would be stronger if “generation” were explicitly presented as an analytic lens or proposed phase transition rather than something the historical evidence has already established.

## 4. Adequacy of the evidence and treatment of the companion study

The manuscript is appropriately explicit that it is **“a framework and a vocabulary rather than a new empirical study,”** and Section 7 similarly states that the companion study establishes feasibility for one task rather than validating the framework; this is an important and appropriately cautious qualification. Nevertheless, the companion study currently carries considerable rhetorical weight because it is the only concrete technical evidence offered for the proposed layer, while apparently addressing only follow-up instruction extraction in a controlled benchmark. Success on action–time pairing can support the tractability of one component of ACR construction, but it cannot establish general recovery of patient-specific intent across referrals, handoffs, medication transitions, conditional escalation, patient self-management, multilingual communication, conflicting plans, or longitudinal revisions. The Perspective should therefore separate three claims more explicitly: the conceptual usefulness of the framework, technical feasibility of one narrow extraction problem, and eventual clinical utility/safety of executable ACRs. In particular, no evidence presented here demonstrates that ACR-based systems improve loop closure, patient safety, workflow burden, or clinical outcomes, and those endpoints should remain prospective hypotheses.

## 5. Fit as a JAMIA Perspective versus Research and Applications

The manuscript fits **JAMIA Perspective** better than Research and Applications because its primary contribution is a conceptual reframing, terminology, proposed representation, evaluation agenda, and set of research hypotheses rather than a completed empirical evaluation. The paper appropriately includes a research agenda and explicitly acknowledges the prospective status of the third layer, which are features compatible with a Perspective. However, the manuscript should lean further into that genre by making the central intellectual argument more rigorous and reducing any appearance that a companion technical experiment validates the broad thesis. There is also an editorial inconsistency requiring correction: the HTML title identifies the article as “JAMIA Research and Applications,” whereas the manuscript itself states that it is submitted as a Perspective. Given the 2000-word constraint, space should be concentrated on defending the conceptual boundary and significance of ACR rather than providing a compressed quasi-history of healthcare IT.

## 6. Most likely reviewer objections, with concrete revisions

**First, “this is not a new computational layer; it is NLP that populates existing workflow resources.”** Address this by defining the transformation that is absent from current infrastructure—communication → source-grounded patient-specific intent → executable workflow—and giving explicit counterexamples showing why ordinary entity/relation extraction or direct FHIR representation is insufficient. **Second, “the three generations are an arbitrary historical taxonomy.”** Recast the model as a proposed computational-object framework, soften historical inevitability language, and state criteria by which another researcher could reject the proposed third-layer classification. **Third, “ACR duplicates FHIR Task/ServiceRequest/CarePlan.”** Add a compact field-by-field mapping demonstrating which ACR properties correspond to existing resources, which are extraction/provenance metadata, and why ACR exists upstream rather than as a competing standard. **Fourth, “the empirical support is too narrow.”** Explicitly label the companion result as an existence proof for one subproblem and identify the empirical tests still required on authentic multi-institutional communication before the broader framework is supported. **Fifth, “automatically executing inferred clinical intent is unsafe.”** Distinguish extraction, representation, clinician-confirmed actionability, and autonomous execution more sharply, and make human confirmation the expected operating mode until task-specific calibration and prospective safety evidence justify higher automation.

## 7. Weaknesses in clarity and literature positioning

The manuscript is generally readable, but several terms carry more conceptual weight than their definitions currently justify: “generation,” “layer,” “clinical intent,” “actionable,” “executable,” “workflow,” and “care process” need sharper boundaries, especially because “intent” may refer to a clinician's instruction, a negotiated patient–clinician plan, a standing order, or an inferred future action. The paper also sometimes creates an unnecessarily sharp contrast between clinical NLP as producing “facts” and the proposed layer as producing “actions”; prior NLP work includes relations, temporality, events, plans, recommendations, and future-oriented information, so the distinction should be framed around **operational workflow semantics and execution requirements**, not facts versus actions alone. Literature positioning is currently strongest around EHRs, FHIR, guidelines, process mining, and classic NLP, but weaker around clinical plan/intent extraction, task and workflow representations, longitudinal care coordination, order and recommendation extraction, human factors, and sociotechnical studies of responsibility and handoffs; those bodies of work are particularly important for demonstrating that ACR fills an identifiable gap. Finally, the manuscript should remove small signals of submission-stage inconsistency, especially the Research-and-Applications/Perspective labeling conflict, and ensure that every broad claim about what existing systems “do not provide” is formulated as a precise capability boundary rather than a categorical absence.
