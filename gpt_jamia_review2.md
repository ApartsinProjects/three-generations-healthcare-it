## (1) Updated recommendation: Minor revision

**Recommendation: Minor revision.** The revision resolves the central objections sufficiently that I would no longer recommend major revision. The manuscript now presents the third layer as a prospective analytic hypothesis rather than an established historical fact, explicitly acknowledges that FHIR and related workflow formalisms can represent patient-specific intent once structured, and localizes the proposed gap to recovering such intent from ordinary clinical communication. The ACR is correspondingly positioned as an upstream, source-grounded intermediate representation that can populate existing workflow infrastructure rather than as a competing clinical information standard.

The remaining issues are mostly precision problems rather than defects requiring a new conceptual architecture or new empirical evidence. I would, however, require one final pass on the ACR/FHIR boundary and on the semantics of the proposed ACR “status”/maturity ladder before acceptance.

## (2) Status of the four prior concerns

### (a) ACR was not clearly distinct from FHIR Task/ServiceRequest/CarePlan — ADEQUATELY RESOLVED, with one technical qualification

This concern is substantially resolved. Section 5.2 now provides the missing conceptual mapping: action/target map toward Task or ServiceRequest and their codes, temporal constraints toward occurrence timing, actor toward ownership, dependencies toward basedOn/partOf relationships, and status toward lifecycle representation. Most importantly, the manuscript explicitly states that the ACR sits **upstream** of FHIR workflow resources and that existing systems can execute instructions once they have been structured; the proposed layer instead addresses recovery from communication. Figure 3 reinforces this architecture.

This is a much more defensible novelty claim. The ACR need not be a superior workflow representation to FHIR in order to be useful; its contribution can be the source-grounded representation produced during communication-to-workflow translation.

One sentence should nevertheless be corrected: the claim that “provenance and confidence are extraction metadata with no native slot” is too categorical. In particular, FHIR has mechanisms for provenance outside the immediate Task/ServiceRequest field set, and extensions can represent additional metadata. The intended claim appears to be that these are **properties of the extraction/recovery process rather than native semantic fields of the target workflow resource**. That formulation would preserve the distinction without making an unnecessarily vulnerable standards claim.

### (b) Boundary claim about existing standards and intent was overstated — ADEQUATELY RESOLVED IN SUBSTANCE

The revised wording gets the central distinction right. Section 4 explicitly says that FHIR represents patient-specific intent, computer-interpretable guidelines represent executable protocols, and process mining reconstructs observed pathways. The manuscript then identifies the missing capability as recovering intended process from ordinary communication and converting it into computable form. This is considerably stronger and more accurate than asserting that existing standards cannot represent intent.

There remains a small internal inconsistency. After acknowledging that FHIR represents patient-specific intent, Section 4 says that “Existing paradigms address the first two” of prescribed, observed, and intended process, while Table 2 assigns “proposed third layer” as the existing paradigm for intended process. That is still too exclusive: existing workflow resources clearly address *represented* intended process; what they do not themselves supply is the communication-to-structured-intent recovery capability being proposed here.

I recommend changing the relevant sentence to something like “existing paradigms provide representations for intended process once structured, but do not generally recover it from unstructured clinical communication,” and revising Table 2 so that the intended row distinguishes **representation/operationalization** from **recovery from communication**. This is a minor but important consistency fix.

### (c) Companion feasibility study carried too much rhetorical weight — ADEQUATELY RESOLVED

This concern is now clearly resolved. The Introduction explicitly describes the Perspective as “a framework and a vocabulary rather than a new empirical study.” More importantly, Section 7 calls the companion result a “formative feasibility test,” limits it to one minimal follow-up-extraction task, and explicitly states that it is “an existence proof for one narrow subproblem, not evidence for the framework.”

That is the appropriate evidentiary role for the companion study in a Perspective. I would make only one minor consistency change: the abstract currently says that the companion study “supports tractability,” which is marginally stronger than the careful language in Section 7. “Illustrates tractability for one narrow subproblem” would align the abstract exactly with the body.

### (d) Literature positioning was weak — PARTIALLY RESOLVED

The revision is materially better. It now acknowledges established clinical NLP for concepts, relations, events and temporality; recommendation extraction; structured order extraction from doctor–patient conversation; computer-interpretable guidelines; clinical pathways; process mining; ambient documentation; LLM-based clinical information extraction; temporal normalization; and automation-bias/human-review considerations. The manuscript therefore no longer reads as though communication-derived action extraction begins with the proposed ACR. The expanded bibliography likewise includes recommendation extraction, temporal-relation work, MEDIQA-OE medical-order extraction, ambient AI, and recent clinical-LLM workflow literature.

I would still call this only partially resolved because the coverage remains uneven relative to the manuscript's strongest claims. The workflow/task-representation discussion relies heavily on a general FHIR citation rather than literature or specifications specifically supporting the detailed Task/ServiceRequest/CarePlan semantics invoked in Section 5.2. Sociotechnical handoffs, responsibility transfer, and task ownership are still represented more indirectly than directly. In addition, the sentence in Section 5.2 claiming that “patient messages, incidental-finding follow-up, and ambient documentation” demonstrate actionable content is supported immediately by only two references, one on ambient documentation and one on medical-order extraction; those citations do not obviously substantiate all three categories as written.

For a 2000-word Perspective, an exhaustive review is neither possible nor desirable, so I would not require a large new related-work section. A few more precisely matched citations, and tightening claims to what the cited literature actually demonstrates, would suffice.

## (3) Any NEW or remaining weaknesses

The most important new conceptual issue is the statement that the six-rung capability ladder—“mentioned, interpreted, actionable, executable, monitored, and closed”—is the ACR's **status attribute**. These labels appear to combine at least two different dimensions. “Mentioned/interpreted/actionable/executable” describe representational completeness or execution readiness, whereas “monitored/closed” describe workflow lifecycle state after an action has entered operation. A record can, for example, be fully executable but pending, in progress, completed, cancelled, or overdue. I recommend separating **maturity/readiness** from **workflow status**. This would also make the subsequent mapping of “status” onto FHIR lifecycle semantics substantially cleaner.

Second, the proposed falsification criterion in Section 8 is intellectually welcome but not yet a strong rejection criterion for the conceptual framework. Failure of machine-actionable FHIR use to increase, or failure of ambient systems to progress from notes toward tasks and orders, could result from reimbursement, regulation, organizational incentives, liability, usability, or vendor strategy rather than from the absence of a distinct computational problem. A stronger falsifier would be conceptual/empirical: for example, if existing clinical-information representations and standard extraction metrics prove sufficient to represent and evaluate communication-derived executable intent without requiring the ACR distinctions or executable-correctness criteria, then the proposed layer has not identified a genuinely distinct computational object.

Third, the evaluation framework usefully separates action detection, argument extraction, linking, temporal normalization, unsupported actions, omissions, calibration, provenance, and loop closure. However, “loop-closure outcome” is qualitatively different from most of the preceding metrics: it is a downstream sociotechnical/clinical-system outcome affected by implementation, staffing, patient behavior, and workflow design, not simply by ACR-recovery correctness. The manuscript should explicitly distinguish **representation/extraction evaluation** from **downstream operational impact evaluation**.

Finally, the manuscript's use of “generation” is now much safer because Section 2 identifies the first two layers as retrospective abstractions and the third as a prospective hypothesis. Nevertheless, “third generation” remains rhetorically stronger than the evidence establishes. I would retain the terminology because this is a Perspective, but consistently describe it as a proposed computational layer or analytic lens rather than implying an empirically established historical transition.

I found no obvious format problem: the structured abstract is essentially at the stated 150-word limit, and the main prose appears to remain within the approximately 2000-word Perspective constraint when references, tables, and figure material are excluded.

## (4) The single most important thing to change before acceptance

**Make the ACR/FHIR boundary completely precise and internally consistent.** The manuscript's strongest defensible claim is not that existing healthcare standards lack representations for patient-specific intent, nor that the ACR replaces those representations. It is that a distinct computational problem lies **upstream**: recovering source-grounded, temporally and conditionally resolved, uncertainty-aware intended actions from ordinary clinical communication and translating them into representations that existing workflow infrastructure can execute.

I would revise Section 4, Table 2, and the key paragraph of Section 5.2 together so that they state exactly this distinction without exceptions or contradictory shorthand. At the same time, distinguish ACR extraction/readiness metadata from downstream FHIR workflow lifecycle state. Once that boundary is made technically exact, the central conceptual contribution is sufficiently clear and appropriately scoped for a JAMIA Perspective, and I would be comfortable with acceptance after minor revision.
